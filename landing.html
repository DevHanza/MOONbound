Landing on the Moon

Why the Sun and the Earth in the lunar sky decide where we land.

The short version

Landing on the Moon is only the first problem. A lander then has to stay powered, stay warm and stay in touch, and all three depend on one thing: where the Sun and the Earth sit above the horizon at that spot, on that day.

Three things decide a landing

Mission planners weigh many things. These three are the ones MOONbound helps you compare.

01

Sunlight

Power and heat

A lunar day lasts about 29.5 Earth days: roughly two weeks of sunlight, then two of night. Surfaces swing from about 127 °C in sunlight to −173 °C in the dark, so a lander must finish its work in daylight or survive the cold.

The Moon’s axis is tilted only about 1.5°, so near the poles the Sun skims the horizon. Some ridges there stay lit for most of the year, while the floors of deep craters never see the Sun.

02

Earth in the sky

Radio contact

The Moon always shows Earth the same face, so from the near side Earth hangs in almost the same spot in the sky and a lander has a direct radio line home, about 1.3 seconds away.

It is not perfectly still: the Moon’s slow wobble, called libration, shifts Earth by several degrees. Near the poles Earth sits low and hills can hide it, and on the far side it never rises, so a relay satellite is needed.

03

The ground

Slopes, rocks and craters

Gentle slopes and few boulders decide where a lander can stand. Orbiters such as NASA’s LRO map the ground to within metres, and the Apollo sites were picked from such images and from earlier robotic scouts.

The terrain also draws the skyline. A ridge a few kilometres away can shade the Sun or block Earth, which is why MOONbound scans the horizon around every site.

Landings so far

From the first robot to the first footprints, and on to a crowded south pole.

  1. Feb 1966

    Luna 9

    The Soviet Union’s probe made the first soft landing on the Moon and sent back the first pictures from the surface.

  2. 1966 – 1968

    Surveyor

    Five NASA landers scouted the ground and tested how firm it was, so Apollo knew a spacecraft would not sink.

  3. Jul 1969

    Apollo 11

    Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin landed in the Sea of Tranquility, the first people on the Moon. LRO has since photographed the site from orbit, and the lander’s descent stage is easy to spot.

  4. Nov 1969

    Apollo 12

    The crew landed within walking distance of Surveyor 3, about 180 metres away, proving a pinpoint landing was possible. They brought back parts of the old probe.

  5. Dec 1972

    Apollo 17

    The last crewed landing, in the Taurus-Littrow valley. Harrison Schmitt, the only professional geologist to walk on the Moon, studied this split boulder. In all, twelve people walked on the Moon and brought back about 382 kg of rock and soil.

  6. 2013 – 2019

    China’s Chang’e missions

    Chang’e 3 made the first soft landing in almost four decades. In January 2019 Chang’e 4 became the first to land on the far side, talking to Earth through a relay satellite.

  7. 2023 – 2025

    A new wave of landers

    India’s Chandrayaan-3 landed near the south pole in August 2023, and Japan’s SLIM made a precision landing in January 2024. Commercial landers followed: Intuitive Machines’ Odysseus in February 2024 and Firefly’s Blue Ghost in March 2025. Next come the Artemis landings, aimed at the south pole.
